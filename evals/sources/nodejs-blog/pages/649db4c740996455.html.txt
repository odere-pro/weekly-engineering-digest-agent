            <h1 id="nodejs-interactive-2026-a-recap">
              <a href="#nodejs-interactive-2026-a-recap">Node.js Interactive 2026: A Recap</a>
            </h1>
            <p>
              More than a decade after
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/en/blog/announcements/interactive-2015"
                >the first Node.js Interactive was announced</a
              >, the conference returned on August 12 and 13, 2026, as a dedicated experience inside
              <a href="https://www.renderatl.com/node">RenderATL</a> and alongside
              <a href="https://atl.tech/">Atlanta Tech Week</a>.
            </p>
            <p>
              Across the
              <a href="https://openjsf.org/blog/node-interactive-speakers-2026"
                >two-day speaker program</a
              >, attendees learned that the future of Node.js is more than its extensive APIs. It
              also depends on the people who maintain critical infrastructure, the standards that
              keep runtimes interoperable, the platforms that make safe development easier, and the
              documentation and tests that turn new ideas into dependable software.
            </p>
            <p>
              Here are the themes that stood out and the takeaways we think every Node.js-er should
              know.
            </p>
            <h2 id="open-source-infrastructure-still-runs-on-people">
              <a href="#open-source-infrastructure-still-runs-on-people"
                >Open source infrastructure still runs on people</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              <a href="https://github.com/rginn">Robin Bender Ginn</a>, Executive Director of the
              <a href="https://openjsf.org/">OpenJS Foundation</a>, opened the conference with her
              talk, <strong>&quot;Node.js Runs AI. Who Runs Node.js?&quot;</strong>:
            </p>
            <p>
              The JavaScript ecosystem powers an enormous share of the modern internet, including
              much of the infrastructure behind the current AI wave. But projects such as
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/">Node.js</a>,
              <a href="https://expressjs.com/">Express</a>,
              <a href="https://www.electronjs.org/">Electron</a>, and
              <a href="https://webpack.js.org/">webpack</a> do not maintain themselves. They depend
              on people, often surprisingly small teams, doing the ongoing work of reviewing
              changes, responding to security reports, cutting releases, improving documentation,
              and keeping communities healthy.
            </p>
            <p>
              Robin described those maintainers as part of the &quot;missing middle&quot;: essential
              to millions of developers and businesses, but too easily treated as an invisible
              resource. Her session focused on what sustainable maintainer support looks like and
              what developers and companies can do to strengthen the projects they rely on.
            </p>
            <p>
              Open source also depends on people agreeing about how independently developed systems
              should work together. In
              <strong
                >&quot;Code, Consensus, and Community: How the JS Ecosystem Continues To Move
                Forward Together,&quot;</strong
              >
              <a href="https://github.com/joesepi">Joe Sepi</a>, Workers Engineering Manager at
              <a href="https://www.cloudflare.com/">Cloudflare</a>, walked through the
              &quot;alphabet soup&quot; of JavaScript governance, including the
              <a href="https://www.w3.org/">W3C</a>,
              <a href="https://ecma-international.org/">Ecma International</a>, and the OpenJS
              Foundation.
            </p>
            <p>
              For instance, the
              <a href="https://wintercg.org/">Web-interoperable Runtimes Community Group</a>, or
              WinterCG, began as a grassroots effort to align APIs across Node.js,
              <a href="https://deno.land">Deno</a>,
              <a href="https://www.cloudflare.com/products/workers/">Cloudflare Workers</a>, and
              other server runtimes. Today, it has moved into a formal standardization track as
              <a href="https://ecma-international.org/technical-committees/tc55/">Ecma TC55</a>, or
              WinterTC. Its initial goal is to define a verifiable minimum common API for
              server-side JavaScript environments, letting runtimes become more interoperable
              without requiring them to become identical.
            </p>
            <h2 id="the-supply-chain-is-an-identity-problem">
              <a href="#the-supply-chain-is-an-identity-problem"
                >The supply chain is an identity problem</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              In <strong>&quot;AI Slop and the Vulnerability Treadmill,&quot;</strong>
              <a href="https://redmonk.com/kholterhoff/">Kate Holterhoff</a> traced our past year,
              in which package security repeatedly became equal to account security.
            </p>
            <p>
              In September 2025, the self-replicating
              <a
                href="https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/"
                >Shai-Hulud attack</a
              >
              spread through compromised npm maintainer accounts. GitHub ultimately removed more
              than 500 compromised packages from the registry. A second wave in November
              <a href="https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/shai-hulud-2.0-npm-worm/"
                >backdoored at least 796 packages</a
              >, representing more than 20 million weekly downloads, and exfiltrated stolen
              credentials through public GitHub repositories.
            </p>
            <p>
              Then, in March 2026, an attacker compromised an Axios maintainer account and
              <a
                href="https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/axios-npm-supply-chain-compromise/"
                >published malicious releases of a package with more than 100 million weekly
                downloads</a
              >. The legitimate release had used OIDC-based trusted publishing through GitHub
              Actions. The attacker bypassed that path and published directly from the compromised
              account.
            </p>
            <p>
              While the incidents differed in execution, they generally followed a similar chain of
              attack: compromise a human identity, then use its legitimate publishing rights to move
              downstream. That is why defenses such as
              <a href="https://docs.npmjs.com/trusted-publishers">npm trusted publishing</a>,
              short-lived credentials, phishing-resistant authentication, protected recovery paths,
              dependency cooldowns, and tightly scoped automation all matter now more than ever.
            </p>
            <p>
              Additionally, AI now sits on both sides of that struggle. It makes convincing social
              engineering and high-volume, low-quality vulnerability reports cheaper to produce. In
              January, the curl project
              <a href="https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/"
                >ended its monetary bug bounty</a
              >
              after its rate of confirmed reports fell below five percent. The project
              <a href="https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/02/25/curl-security-moves-again/"
                >later returned to HackerOne as an intake platform</a
              >, but without monetary rewards.
            </p>
            <p>
              The same broad class of technology can help defenders. Security researchers at AISLE
              used an AI-driven system to
              <a
                href="https://aisle.com/newsroom/press-releases/aisle-finds-12-vulnerabilities-in-openssl"
                >identify all 12 vulnerabilities addressed in OpenSSL&#x27;s January 2026 security
                release</a
              >. Human researchers validated the findings, worked through responsible disclosure,
              and collaborated with OpenSSL on remediation.
              <a href="https://aisle.com/blog/aisle-discovers-20-openssl-zero-days-in-6-months"
                >Some of the underlying bugs had remained in the codebase for more than 25 years</a
              >.
            </p>
            <h2 id="reproducibility-needs-more-than-version-numbers">
              <a href="#reproducibility-needs-more-than-version-numbers"
                >Reproducibility needs more than version numbers</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              Supply-chain reliability is also shaped by how package managers interpret metadata. In
              <strong>&quot;Beyond SemVer,&quot;</strong>
              <a href="https://x.com/darcy">Darcy Clarke</a>, founder and CEO of
              <a href="https://vlt.sh/">vlt</a> discussed the future of versioning packages and the
              interpretation of package metadata.
            </p>
            <p>
              <a href="https://semver.org/">Semantic Versioning 2.0.0</a> defines the structure and
              precedence of versions. It does not, however, define the complete range language
              people use in <code>package.json</code> files. Carets, tildes, and many advanced range
              expressions are conventions implemented by tools such as
              <a href="https://www.npmjs.com/package/semver"><code>node-semver</code></a
              >.
            </p>
            <p>
              Darcy focused on another frequently overlooked part of the specification: build
              metadata. SemVer intentionally ignores build metadata when calculating version
              precedence. Rather than changing that rule, his talk explored using build metadata as
              a backwards-compatible extension point for richer package information. He proposed a
              brand new variant of the SemVer specification, open-source and available at
              <a href="https://semver.xyz">semver.xyz</a>.
            </p>
            <h2 id="more-code-needs-stronger-platforms">
              <a href="#more-code-needs-stronger-platforms">More code needs stronger platforms</a>
            </h2>
            <p>
              While AI coding tools can increase how quickly a team produces code, they do not
              change what makes that code safe to ship.
            </p>
            <p>
              In <strong>&quot;Platform Thinking for the AI Era,&quot;</strong>
              <a href="https://bekah.io/">Bekah Suttner Cheek</a>, Staff Software Engineer at
              <a href="https://www.fastly.com/">Fastly</a>, argued that familiar platform
              fundamentals become more important as development speeds up: fast CI, meaningful
              tests, safe rollbacks, healthy codebase patterns, and clear paths from a local change
              to production.
            </p>
            <p>
              Despite the name, platform thinking is not exclusive to people with
              &quot;platform&quot; in their title. Everyone who designs a workflow, API, test suite,
              or deployment process can make the safe choice easier. Good guardrails prevent common
              mistakes, surface failures quickly, and make recovery routine instead of heroic. They
              also treat a near miss as useful evidence. Waiting for the same weakness to become a
              full incident is an expensive way to learn.
            </p>
            <p>
              This is ultimately a trust problem. Users and internal teams should not need to
              remember hidden rules or jump through unnecessary hoops to do the right thing. The
              platform should guide them there by default.
            </p>
            <h2 id="nodejs-is-becoming-more-capable-out-of-the-box">
              <a href="#nodejs-is-becoming-more-capable-out-of-the-box"
                >Node.js is becoming more capable out of the box</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              Not long ago, starting a Node.js project often meant installing a collection of
              packages before writing application code. Environment variables, tests, HTTP clients,
              file watching, and TypeScript support all commonly began in userland.
            </p>
            <p>
              In
              <strong>&quot;The New Node.js: Built-in Batteries and the Road Ahead,&quot;</strong>
              <a href="https://nodeland.dev/">Matteo Collina</a>, Co-Founder and CTO of
              <a href="https://platformatic.dev/">Platformatic</a>, showed how much of that
              foundation now ships with the runtime. Modern Node.js includes built-in TypeScript
              type stripping,
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/test.html"><code>node:test</code></a> and
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/assert.html"><code>node:assert</code></a
              >, native <code>.env</code> loading,
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/globals.html#fetch"><code>fetch</code></a> powered by
              <a href="https://undici.nodejs.org/">Undici</a>, watch mode,
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/sqlite.html"><code>node:sqlite</code></a
              >, and the stable
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/permissions.html">Permission Model</a> (among a great
              many other things).
            </p>
            <p>
              However, despite all this addition, adoption remains the harder part. Teams still run
              unsupported versions because upgrades require time, testing, and coordination.
              That&#x27;s why, starting with the 27.x line,
              <a
                href="https://nodejs.org/en/blog/announcements/evolving-the-nodejs-release-schedule"
                >Node.js will move from two major releases per year to one</a
              >. An Alpha phase begins in October 2026, Node.js 27.0.0 becomes Current in April
              2027, and it enters LTS in October 2027. Version numbers will align with the calendar
              year of their initial Current release, and every release will eventually become LTS.
              This change reduces the number of concurrent release lines maintainers must support
              while preserving a predictable testing and migration window for users.
            </p>
            <h2 id="quic-and-http3-are-taking-shape-in-nodejs">
              <a href="#quic-and-http3-are-taking-shape-in-nodejs"
                >QUIC and HTTP/3 are taking shape in Node.js</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              <a href="https://www.jasnell.me/">James Snell</a>, System Engineer at Cloudflare, has
              led much of Node.js&#x27;s work on HTTP/2, QUIC, and HTTP/3. In
              <strong>&quot;QUIC in Node.js,&quot;</strong> he reviewed an implementation that has
              gone through several major iterations since the effort began in 2018.
            </p>
            <p>
              QUIC and HTTP/3 are related, but they are not interchangeable names.
              <a href="https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000">QUIC</a> is a secure, multiplexed
              transport protocol built on UDP.
              <a href="https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9114">HTTP/3</a> maps HTTP semantics onto
              QUIC.
            </p>
            <p>
              Node.js&#x27;s
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/quic.html"><code>node:quic</code></a> implementation
              is still in active development. Using it requires a Node.js binary built with
              experimental QUIC support and then starting that binary with
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/api/cli.html#--experimental-quic"
                ><code>--experimental-quic</code></a
              >. The runtime flag cannot add QUIC support to a binary that was compiled without it.
            </p>
            <p>
              James also discussed the work needed to move the implementation forward, including
              tests, documentation, and the use of AI to help make progress on a change set whose
              scale had previously stalled development. Completing the work would allow more of
              Node.js&#x27;s networking stack, including <code>fetch</code>, to benefit from HTTP/3
              over time.
            </p>
            <p>
              The session also previewed a proposed unified server API spanning HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2,
              and HTTP/3, with the intent to bring the design to WinterTC for broader discussion,
              something Node.js &amp; James are both very excited about.
            </p>
            <h2 id="documentation-and-testing-are-infrastructure">
              <a href="#documentation-and-testing-are-infrastructure"
                >Documentation and testing are infrastructure</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              New runtime features only matter when developers can understand and trust them. That
              makes documentation and testing infrastructure, not supporting material.
            </p>
            <p>
              In
              <strong
                >&quot;Replacing What Works: doc-kit and the Next 10 Years of Node.js
                Documentation,&quot;</strong
              >
              <a href="https://github.com/bmuenzenmeyer">Brian Muenzenmeyer</a> and
              <a href="https://github.com/ovflowd">Claudio Wunder</a> told the story of replacing a
              documentation pipeline that dates back to Node.js v0.6.
            </p>
            <p>
              The new <a href="https://github.com/nodejs/doc-kit"><code>doc-kit</code></a> CLI
              parses, lints, and transforms Markdown into an annotated representation that can
              produce redesigned web pages, legacy HTML, man pages, JSON schemas, search indexes,
              and <code>llms.txt</code> files. Keeping those outputs aligned is one of the major
              advantages of moving them onto a shared pipeline.
            </p>
            <p>
              The migration also brings a redesigned reader experience shaped by research from the
              Node.js website and infrastructure teams. You can
              <a href="https://beta.docs.nodejs.org/">preview the new API documentation</a> today
              and read more about
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/en/blog/announcements/new-api-docs-beta"
                >the design and the tooling behind it</a
              >. The beta remains usable without JavaScript and offline, while adding faster
              navigation, improved search, and clearer information architecture.
            </p>
            <p>
              Testing the systems described by that documentation can be just as difficult.
              Local-only failures, operating-system differences, port conflicts, and CI constraints
              all make integration tests harder to run reliably and in parallel.
            </p>
            <p>
              In
              <strong
                >&quot;Performant, Parallelizable, Framework Agnostic Node.js Integration
                Testing,&quot;</strong
              >
              <a href="https://github.com/Ethan-Arrowood">Ethan Arrowood</a>, Head of Open Source
              Engineering at <a href="https://harper.fast/">Harper</a>, presented
              <a href="https://www.npmjs.com/package/@harperfast/integration-testing"
                ><code>@harperfast/integration-testing</code></a
              >. The framework runs real processes, allocates ports dynamically, supports parallel
              execution, and integrates with both the Node.js test runner and external runners like
              Playwright.
            </p>
            <p>
              Integration tests, like the ones Ethan showed us, verify that the pieces of an
              application work together as a system. Good test tooling makes that level of
              confidence repeatable across local machines, operating systems, and CI rather than
              reserving it for the one environment where everything happens to line up.
            </p>
            <h2 id="ai-may-change-the-workflow-but-not-the-responsibility">
              <a href="#ai-may-change-the-workflow-but-not-the-responsibility"
                >AI may change the workflow, but not the responsibility</a
              >
            </h2>
            <p>
              Several sessions approached AI from different layers and views of the software stack,
              but ultimately, all asserted that developers still own the systems they ship.
            </p>
            <p>
              In
              <strong
                >&quot;Node.js After the AI Shift: Building Tools Developers Can
                Trust,&quot;</strong
              >
              <a href="https://github.com/AndreaGriffiths11">Andrea Griffiths</a>, Senior Developer
              Advocate at <a href="https://github.com/">GitHub</a>, broke AI-powered Node.js
              features into practical components: inputs, context, tool calls, guardrails,
              evaluation, and developer experience.
            </p>
            <p>
              <a href="https://aileenvl.com/">Aileen Villanueva Lecuona</a>, a Senior Software
              Engineer and Google Developer Expert, made those boundaries more concrete in
              <strong
                >&quot;Guiding AI Agents with MCP, Skills and Spec-Driven Development for Reliable
                Node.js Backends.&quot;</strong
              >
              Her workflow combined the
              <a href="https://modelcontextprotocol.io/">Model Context Protocol</a>, specialized
              skills, and specifications that act as the source of truth.
            </p>
            <p>
              In <strong>&quot;Imagineering Future Interfaces,&quot;</strong>
              <a href="https://charliegerard.dev/">Charlie Gerard</a>, Senior Research Engineer at
              <a href="https://www.crowdstrike.com/">CrowdStrike</a>, shifted the focus from control
              to creativity. AI is often good at reproducing patterns that already exist. Humans
              remain responsible for imagining what has not been built yet. Her examples showed how
              agents can help product developers to prototype new interfaces based on previous human
              interaction in order to improve user experience.
            </p>
            <p>
              Finally, in <strong>&quot;Client Performance in the Age of AI,&quot;</strong>
              <a href="https://jenna.is/">Jenna Zeigen</a>, Senior Staff Engineer at
              <a href="https://www.notion.so/">Notion</a>, argued that generating more code does not
              guarantee generating better patterns. In fact, it creates more surface area for
              regressions and more opportunities for users to feel the accumulated cost.
            </p>
            <p>
              However, there is a solution to this: <em>specific</em> metrics, flamegraphs, and
              representative benchmarks. For example,
              <a href="https://web.dev/articles/inp">Interaction to Next Paint</a> measures whether
              an interface responds promptly when someone clicks, taps, or types. Another metric,
              Time to First Token measures how long an AI-backed experience takes to begin
              responding. No single metric tells the entire story, which is why choosing the right
              benchmarks is just as important as having one.
            </p>
            <h2 id="code--learn"><a href="#code--learn">Code &amp; Learn</a></h2>
            <p>
              Finally, Node.js Interactive also ended the conference with a Code &amp; Learn session
              in partnership with <a href="https://harper.fast/">Harper</a>. Attendees at every
              experience level sat down with core maintainers and worked through a curated set of
              ready-to-go contributions to Node.js core.
            </p>
            <p>
              The session began with the ethos of open source, the project&#x27;s contribution
              guidelines, responsible use of AI-assisted development, and practical advice for
              finding an issue to work on. From there, participants moved into reproducing problems,
              changing code, adding tests, receiving review, and submitting patches. Plus, everyone
              who submitted a contribution also walked away with some cool new Node.js swag.
            </p>
            <p>
              The event may be over, but the
              <a href="https://nodejs.org/en/contribute">Node.js contribution guide</a> is always
              open, and we&#x27;d love to have you contribute to the future of open source software.
            </p>
