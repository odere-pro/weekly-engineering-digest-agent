              <section class="relative w-full">
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  A few months ago, our team investigated a suspected memory leak in vLLM. At first,
                  we thought the issue would be easy to spot, something confined to the upper layers
                  of the codebase. But the deeper we looked, the more complex it became. This
                  article kicks off our new Engineering Deep Dive series, where we’ll share how we
                  tackle technical investigations and build solutions at Mistral AI.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  The issue first appeared during pre-production testing of disaggregated serving
                  with one of our
                  <a
                    href="https://docs.mistral.ai/getting-started/models"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >frontier models</a
                  >. Memory usage was climbing steadily, but only under specific conditions: with
                  vLLM, with our
                  <a
                    href="https://docs.mistral.ai/models/mistral-medium-3-1-25-08"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >model Mistral Medium 3.1</a
                  >, and graph compilation enabled. There were no crashes or errors, just a slow
                  linear increase in system memory of 400 MB per minute on production-like traffic. 
                  After a few hours, that would lead to an &quot;out of memory&quot; state.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  What followed was a methodical hunt, starting from high-level Python tools and
                  descending into kernel-level tracing, until we finally uncovered the true source.
                  Here’s how we tracked it down, and what it revealed about the hidden risks of
                  dependencies layers in today’s software.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  <figure class="mb-10">

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  <em>Not the kind of trending upwards graph we like to see on Grafana </em>
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>A leak that played hide and seek</strong>.
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Initially, our approach followed a standard troubleshooting path: we aimed to
                  isolate the source of the leak by replicating the issue on a smaller model, with
                  fewer production optimizations activated. But after trying different settings and
                  models we couldn’t reproduce that on another setup. The error was only present on
                  a Prefill/Decode disaggregated setup with NIXL. 
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Given the central role Prefill/Decode (P/D) disaggregation plays in our story, let
                  us walk you through the high-level mechanisms of how this inference setup works.
                  P/D Disaggregated splits the processing of a query into two phases, processed by
                  different instances:
                </p>
                <ol class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-decimal">
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      First the router sends a “prefill request” (by setting
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >max_tokens=1</code
                      >
                      and by setting an empty set of KV Transfer metadata) to a prefill vLLM
                      instance to compute the KVCache of the request.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      On completion, the router transfers KVCache metadata alongside a “decode
                      request” to a decode vLLM instance.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      KVCache transfer is initiated through NIXL and token generation happens on the
                      decode vLLM instance by using and extending the transferred KVCache. 
                    </p>
                  </li>
                </ol>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  The leak was only observed on the decode side of this disaggregated setup,
                  strongly suggesting that KV Cache transfer through NIXL was the root cause of the
                  leak. In our setup <strong>NIXL</strong> relies on <strong>UCX</strong> (Unified
                  Communication X), a high-performance communication library designed for data
                  exchange in distributed systems. UCX enables optimized data transfer over a large
                  set of technologies, including <strong>Infiniband</strong>, a low-latency,
                  high-throughput interconnect technology commonly used in HPC and data centers.
                </p>

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  <em>Overview of a P/D Disaggregated serving deployment.</em>
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  For the remainder of our investigation, we worked in this setup and started with
                  Python memory profiling tools to pinpoint the source of the leak.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  We tried Memray and Guppy 3, but neither showed a leak and everything they allowed
                  us to observe was normal. Attempting to use GDB made the entire process crash. Our
                  vLLM setup was also too heavy for tools like Valgrind, making it impractically
                  slow or even impossible to use.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  It was clear that a more powerful tool was required to track the leak. But before
                  investing more time, we decided to be sure that this leak was reproducible by
                  others. We reached out to the vLLM team by opening an
                  <a
                    href="https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/24264"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >issue on their GitHub repository</a
                  >, which helped confirm we weren&#39;t the only ones seeing this issue and a
                  deeper investigation was warranted.
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>Counting mallocs and frees with Heaptrack.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  In order to better track what was happening, we turned to
                  <a
                    href="https://github.com/KDE/heaptrack"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >Heaptrack</a
                  >: a memory profiler that overrides memory operations like
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >malloc</code
                  >
                  or <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >free</code
                  >
                  and records these events alongside stack traces.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Millian Wolf, the creator of Heaptrack, has written an excellent introductory
                  <a
                    href="https://milianw.de/blog/heaptrack-a-heap-memory-profiler-for-linux.html"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >blog post</a
                  >
                  to get you started with the tool. It’s a two-step process: first run the program
                  with tracing, then interpret the data dumps.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  In order to track the allocations confined in the worker process of vLLM, we set
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >LD_PRELOAD</code
                  >
                  to
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >libheaptrack_preload.so</code
                  >
                  through vLLM to ensure this library is loaded before any other and overrides the
                  behaviours of memory allocating functions, providing us with the data dump.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  We were then able to visualize this data through
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >heaptrack_interpret</code
                  >:
                </p>

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  <em>The memory usage shown with Heaptrack of our vLLM worker</em>
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  At this point, one might question: where is the memory leak? Indeed, the only
                  visible memory increase was due to a lazy NIXL initialization.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  To validate that the leak was indeed happening in this set-up, we ran a vLLM
                  benchmark and created two Heaptrack snapshots using
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >heaptrack_interpret</code
                  >: one at the beginning and one near the end. Although the heap memory itself
                  remained stable, the <strong>peak resident memory (RSS)</strong>, which we will
                  cover in the next section, differed between the two snapshots. This discrepancy
                  was visible in Heaptrack’s summary tab.
                </p>

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This meant the leak was happening outside the heap, and so not part of the memory
                  that Heaptrack analyzes. We needed to change tools to track allocations outside of
                  the heap.
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong
                    >Beyond the heap: understanding resident memory and system allocations.</strong
                  >
                </h2>
                <figure class="mb-10">
                  <img
                    src="https://cms.globalaegis.net/api/media/file/1d04b034-422e-4b3d-8453-f7f7495b4bca.svg"
                    width="1600"
                    height="1873"
                    alt
                    loading="lazy"
                    decoding="async"
                    class="w-full"
                  />
                </figure>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10"></p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  To understand why Heaptrack couldn’t detect the leak, we first need to clarify
                  what the <strong>Resident Set Size (RSS)</strong> actually includes. RSS
                  represents the portion of a process’s memory held in RAM, and it contains more
                  than just the heap. Specifically, it includes:
                </p>
                <ul class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-disc">
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <strong>The heap</strong> is traditionally managed using the legacy
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >sbrk</code
                      >
                      and
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >brk</code
                      >
                      system calls, which adjust or set the program’s break address, the pointer
                      marking the end of the heap segment.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <strong>The stack</strong>, which stores local variables and function call
                      frames.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <strong>Anonymous memory mappings</strong>, which are regions of memory
                      allocated directly via the
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >mmap</code
                      >
                      system call without a file backing. These are often used by custom allocators
                      or by
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >malloc</code
                      >
                      for larger blocks of memory. The addresses of anonymous mappings usually
                      reside
                      <strong>between the heap address space and the stack address space</strong>,
                      in a region known as the <strong>memory mapping segment</strong>.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                </ul>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  While
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >malloc</code
                  >
                  can use <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >sbrk</code
                  >
                  for small allocations, modern implementations typically prefer using
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  with anonymous mappings since it’s more flexible and lets you allocate huge pages
                  (memory pages of 2MB or 1GB depending on your setup).
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Heaptrack only hooks into glibc&#39;s
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >malloc</code
                  >
                  and <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >free</code
                  >
                  functions. This means it can track all traditional heap and anonymous mapping
                  allocated by
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >malloc</code
                  >
                  directly, but it misses memory allocated through direct <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  calls or other system-level mechanisms outside of glibc&#39;s control.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Fortunately, all isn’t lost when it comes to tracking what was happening. The
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >/proc</code
                  >
                  filesystem is a special folder in Linux that serves as a
                  <strong>kernel API</strong>, exposing a virtual interface to interact with running
                  processes, and provides real-time access to process details, such as:
                </p>
                <ul class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-disc">
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >/proc/&lt;pid&gt;/fd</code
                      >, which lists all open file descriptors for a process.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >/proc/&lt;pid&gt;/maps</code
                      >, which shows a detailed map of the process’s memory regions, including heap,
                      stack, shared libraries, and anonymous mappings.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      And many more (a simple
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >ls</code
                      >
                      in
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >/proc/&lt;pid&gt;/</code
                      >
                      lists what is available).
                    </p>
                  </li>
                </ul>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  To continue our investigation, we used the
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >pmap</code
                  >
                  command, which reads
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >/proc/&lt;pid&gt;/maps</code
                  >
                  and presents memory usage in a human-readable format. Our goal was to track
                  changes in memory regions over time, so we ran:
                </p>
                <div class="expressive-code mb-10">
                  <figure class="frame">
                    <figcaption class="header"></figcaption>

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This command runs
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >pmap</code
                  >
                  every second to display extended memory information for a specified PID, skips the
                  header, and sorts the output by memory size, allowing us to focus on the largest
                  memory regions.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  With this command, we observed an interesting pattern: only some anonymous memory
                  mappings were growing over time, and their start addresses were changing. The size
                  of these allocations became huge over time while most others were simply not
                  moving.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10"></p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  <em>The memory pages listed by our </em
                  ><code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >pmap</code
                  ><em>
                    command, sorted by RSS size, made it easy to spot suspicious allocations. The
                    orange dot highlights an example.</em
                  >
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This behavior is characteristic of
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mremap</code
                  >, a system call used to
                  <strong>resize or relocate existing memory regions </strong>without freeing them.
                  Unlike
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >realloc</code
                  >, which operates within the heap and relies on glibc&#39;s memory management,
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mremap</code
                  >
                  works at a lower level and is often used by custom allocators, libraries, or even
                  manual memory management code to dynamically adjust memory layouts.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This pattern could also result from repeated cycles of
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >munmap</code
                  >
                  followed by
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >, where memory is freed and reallocated but the total usage continues to grow,
                  either due to
                  <strong
                    >fragmentation, leaks in custom allocators, or improper resizing logic. </strong
                  >In our case, the changing addresses and growing size strongly suggested that
                  memory was being reallocated but never properly released.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This was our first concrete indication that the leak wasn’t in the heap but in
                  anonymous <strong>memory regions being resized without proper release.</strong>
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>Tracing the leak with BPFtrace.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Our investigation had narrowed the leak down to raw
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  or
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mremap</code
                  >
                  calls, but we needed to confirm which one was responsible. Our first attempt was
                  to use
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >LD_PRELOAD</code
                  >
                  with a
                  <a
                    href="https://gist.github.com/hasB4K/5211529e2a1741c478f253b82c110d69"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >custom small C library</a
                  >
                  that logged every
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  and
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mremap</code
                  >
                  call, since Heaptrack wasn’t doing it, hoping to intercept the ones we cared
                  about. However, this approach had limitations: <strong>not all </strong
                  ><code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap/mremap</code
                  ><strong> go through glibc</strong>. Our custom hooks saw some allocations but
                  they didn&#39;t match the addresses leaking in our
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >pmap</code
                  >
                  output. The leaking regions were still growing, untracked by our
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >LD_PRELOAD</code
                  >
                  hook. This suggested that those allocations were made by manually doing a syscall
                  or that another hooking mechanism was in place.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  To get a full picture, we turned to <strong>BPFtrace</strong>, a tool for
                  real-time tracing of system calls and kernel events. It relies on the Linux
                  kernel’s <strong>eBPF virtual machine</strong>, which executes lightweight,
                  pre-validated bytecode attached to tracepoints or probes, enabling safe and
                  low-overhead analysis. BPFtrace is also used by some Kubernetes tools to detect
                  anomalous or dangerous behaviors in clusters, such as unauthorized access or
                  resource abuse, all without risking kernel stability.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  We also considered using
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >strace</code
                  >, but its reliance on
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >PTRACE</code
                  >
                  made it too slow to effectively analyze the issue at this stage. Instead, we wrote
                  a BPFtrace script to log <strong>every</strong>
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  <strong>and</strong>
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mremap</code

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">

                <ul class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-disc">

                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">

                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"

                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >mremap</code
                      >
                      sounded like the ideal suspect for a memory allocation that kept on growing.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      Even more intriguing, the calls originated from
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >syscall+29</code
                      >
                      aka <a
                        href="https://sourceware.org/git/?p=glibc.git;a=blob;f=sysdeps/unix/sysv/linux/x86_64/syscall.S;hb=5d1cb431aa#l37"
                        target="_blank"
                        rel="noopener noreferrer"
                        class="underline font-semibold"
                        >glibc&#39;s raw syscall wrapper</a
                      >, that lets users perform a raw syscall through an API like:
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >syscall(SYS_mmap, ...)</code
                      >
                    </p>
                  </li>

                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This represented significant progress, but further investigation was required.
                  While BPFtrace showed us the user stack trace of the system call itself (<code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >ustack</code
                  >
                  in their documentation), it only gave us the first element of the user call stack,
                  not the full user-space context leading to the allocation. We could see where the
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  was called, but not the previous callers. That information would have been
                  essential to us, so we tried to understand why we could not get it, and initially
                  thought it was linked to <strong>frame pointers being disabled</strong> in our
                  setup, and decided to investigate in that direction.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  For context, frame pointer is a feature that stores the return address of function
                  calls in a register or memory location, enabling tools to reconstruct the full
                  call stack. Frame pointers were historically disabled as an optimization for most
                  libraries, as they added minor overhead, but modern distributions have begun
                  re-enabling them since the performance gains are now negligible compared to the
                  debugging benefits.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Sadly, switching from Ubuntu 22.04 LTS to Ubuntu 24.04 LTS (which has frame
                  pointers enabled for native libraries) wasn’t enough. This suggested that an
                  already optimized Python dependency, that disabled frame pointers, was the
                  culprit.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  We pondered the situation for a while: which Python package could be doing such
                  direct syscalls, by-passing the usual standard library calls? At this stage, we
                  had two potential culprits:
                </p>
                <ol class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-decimal">
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <strong>UCX (Unified Communication X)</strong>, a high-performance
                      communication library used for accelerated networking and RDMA (Remote Direct
                      Memory Access). UCX is a dependency of NIXL, which vLLM uses for disaggregated
                      serving. UCX is known for its low-level memory optimizations, including custom
                      memory allocators.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      <strong>PyTorch</strong>, which performs its own memory management and
                      optimizations, often bypassing standard allocators for performance. PyTorch’s
                      custom allocations and JIT compilation could also be responsible for the leak.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                </ol>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  With both suspects on the table, we needed a way to dig deeper. At this stage we
                  turned to GDB automation instead.
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>GDB automation to the rescue.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Using GDB earlier in the investigation wasn’t feasible for a simple reason: GDB
                  attaches to an entire process. With vLLM, attaching GDB to the main process would
                  halt all workers, making it impossible to observe the leak in real time. Since the
                  leak didn’t cause a crash, and memory dumps were impractical due to the sheer size
                  of the process. We were stuck.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  However, from our BPFtrace logs, we noticed that every leaking <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  > call originated from the same address. Like we said in the previous section,
                  this address didn’t point to
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  directly, but it was inside glibc’s the
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >syscall</code
                  >
                  thin-wrapper. This function bypasses glibc’s usual
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  wrapper, which explains why our
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >LD_PRELOAD</code
                  >
                  hooks missed it earlier. We tried to use
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >LD_PRELOAD</code
                  >
                  to intercept the glibc’s
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >syscall</code
                  >, but strangely it wasn’t working either… leaving us with almost no way to trace
                  these calls dynamically.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Since the leaking calls always came from the same
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >syscall</code
                  >
                  instruction, we could automate GDB to break only when that specific address was
                  hit. Here’s how we did it:
                </p>
                <ol class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-decimal">
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We set a<strong> conditional breakpoint</strong> on the
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >syscall</code
                      >
                      address, triggering only if the system call number matched
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >SYS_mmap</code
                      >.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We temporarily broke at the exit of the
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >mmap</code
                      >
                      system call to inspect the return value, which is the allocated address, and
                      printed the full stack trace.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We dumped all available context in one go: the return address, the call stack,
                      and any other relevant registers or memory.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We ran this conditional script
                      <strong>for a few seconds during the benchmark</strong> and compared the
                      captured return addresses with our
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >pmap</code
                      >
                      monitoring to confirm whether they matched the known leaking regions.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                </ol>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Here is the script, which can be run through
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >gdb -x gdb_script.txt</code
                  >:
                </p>

                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>Identifying the role of UCX’s memory hooks.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  We shared these findings with the vLLM team, who helped validate and refine our
                  understanding of the issue. Together, we discovered that UCX uses a
                  <a
                    href="https://github.com/openucx/ucx/blob/179a0e728d7ff0109f916f139e9efd4aa3f207e8/src/ucm/mmap/install.c#L354"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >mmap hooking mechanism</a
                  >
                  to optimize memory operations for InfiniBand, including pre-caching data for
                  transfers (a feature called Registration Cache, or RCache). Memory management for
                  InfiniBand is often expensive due to the need for hardware-level memory
                  registration.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  However, this mechanism intercepts <strong>all</strong>
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  <strong>calls by default</strong>, not just those related to UCX or InfiniBand
                  operations. This broad interception explains why our earlier attempts to trace
                  allocations using our
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >LD_PRELOAD</code
                  >
                  based
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  hook failed: UCX
                  <a
                    href="https://github.com/openucx/ucx/blob/efd5356e0aac6a9d1e7f3b9ab4dfa8e4d90b64fc/src/ucm/util/reloc.c#L742"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >dynamically patches</a
                  >
                  the <strong>Global Offset Table (GOT) entries </strong>used by applications to
                  call functions like
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  and
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >munmap</code
                  >. That&#39;s why it was bypassing our earlier hooks entirely. We also found that
                  this hooking mechanism
                  <a
                    href="https://github.com/openucx/ucx/blob/179a0e728d7ff0109f916f139e9efd4aa3f207e8/src/ucm/malloc/malloc_hook.c#L845"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >is automatically disabled</a
                  >
                  when Valgrind is detected, which would have prevented us from using Valgrind for
                  deeper analysis.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  The GOT is a data structure used by the dynamic linker to resolve function calls
                  in dynamically linked libraries. When a program starts, the dynamic linker
                  populates the GOT with the actual addresses of functions, such as
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >, from shared libraries.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Modifying the GOT at runtime is generally considered bad practice because it can
                  introduce instability, make debugging harder, and break assumptions other parts of
                  the program or libraries rely on. But UCX does this for a good reason. Indeed, UCX
                  does this to manage its Registration Cache, which tracks &quot;registered&quot;
                  (or &quot;pinned&quot;) memory. This is memory that has been pinned in place,
                  ensuring its virtual-to-physical address mapping remains fixed. This allows
                  network adapters to transfer data directly between the network fabric and RAM
                  without CPU involvement, which is critical for performance but requires careful
                  handling, as registered memory is a limited resource.
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>Solving the issue.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Now that we knew that the <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  hooking mechanism was in play, we realized that it was actually good news since we
                  could disable it entirely by setting this environment variable <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >UCX_MEM_MMAP_HOOK_MODE=none</code
                  >. It successfully disabled this behavior, <strong
                    >which resolved the memory leak without impacting performance. </strong
                  >The <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  hooks are useful when sending various memory chunks over RDMA, but in vLLM use
                  case, we only need to handle <strong>one large, contiguous memory region</strong>:
                  the entire vLLM KVCache Manager memory. NIXL only needed to register the memory
                  once for its transfers. Therefore, in the vLLM use case, disabling the hooking
                  mechanism was safe and had no negative impact on disaggregated serving
                  performance.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  After discussing this with the UCX team, we learned that UCX does not immediately
                  free memory when <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >munmap</code
                  >
                  is called. Instead, it moves the region to an invalidation queue for later
                  cleanup. This queue is managed by UCX’s memory pool, which dynamically expands to
                  accommodate more entries as needed. As a result, memory regions accumulated
                  without being released, and the growing queue required additional allocations,
                  explaining why <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  was called during <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >munmap</code
                  >
                  operations. As an alternative solution to the memory leak setting the environment
                  variable <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >UCX_RCACHE_MAX_UNRELEASED=1024</code
                  >
                  (the default value is <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >inf</code
                  >) limits the number of unreleased memory regions in the queue, forcing UCX to
                  initiate cleanup once the threshold is reached.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  The thing is, this should not have happened in the first place. NIXL and vLLM were
                  indeed <a
                    href="https://github.com/ai-dynamo/nixl/blob/f41fc127a8ed993908a6cac799728637a7e1f26a/src/plugins/ucx/ucx_backend.cpp#L394"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    ><u>calling</u></a
                  >
                  the <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >ucp_worker_progress()</code
                  >
                  function that should have triggered the memory pool cleanup. Why it was not
                  triggered in this specific edge case is still not clear. But it showed that
                  setting a default value of <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >UCX_RCACHE_MAX_UNRELEASED</code
                  >
                  to infinity was not correct. The UCX and NIXL team decided to change this
                  behavior <a
                    href="https://github.com/ai-dynamo/nixl/pull/1210"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    ><u>for a future NIXL release</u></a
                  >. In the meantime, we merged a <a
                    href="https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/32181"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    ><u>fix</u></a
                  >
                  in the vLLM repository to help the community avoid running into the same leak. 
                </p>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>A summary of the investigation.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">The investigation in brief:</p>
                <ol class="mb-10 pl-5 flex flex-col gap-2 list-decimal">
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We noticed a
                      <strong>rapidly-growing memory leak in our production setting</strong> when
                      deploying one of our frontier models with disaggregated serving.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We attempted to trim down the environment and get a minimal reproducer.
                      Unfortunately,
                      <strong
                        >the bug only reproduced on a complex setup, with a large model, and
                        disaggregation enabled.</strong
                      >
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We turned into Memray, Guppy 3, and Heaptrack to analyze the leak.
                      <strong>Nothing obvious came of these tools.</strong> However, we noticed
                      something peculiar in Heaptrack’s metrics.
                      <strong>The resident memory was unusually large</strong>, so we decided to dig
                      in that direction.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      Leveraging
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >pmap</code
                      >, we were able to see
                      <strong
                        >RSS allocations that kept on growing, with their associated base
                        pointers.</strong
                      >
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We wanted to get more clues about who was doing those calls. With a bit of
                      scripting and the use of BPFtrace,
                      <strong>we found that the leaks originated from </strong
                      ><code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >mmap</code
                      ><strong> calls.</strong> Even with our best efforts, we were not able to
                      collect full stack traces (that would have allowed us to pin-point the culprit
                      call site), but
                      <strong
                        >that led us to believe that the syscalls were made by a heavily optimized
                        package.</strong
                      >
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      Thanks to the gathered information, we were able to
                      <strong
                        >set up very specific GDB breakpoints that would only be triggered by the
                        offending calls.</strong
                      >
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      We found that <strong>UCX was performing those calls.</strong> While the
                      purpose of the calls is legitimate: improve performance for InfiniBand
                      transfers, it created ever-growing allocations in the RSS and
                      <strong>prevented us from deploying disaggregated serving for days.</strong>
                    </p>
                  </li>
                  <li>
                    <p class="text-body-large text-current">
                      Knowing the source of the leak, the work-around was easy to find:
                      <strong>setting</strong>
                      <code
                        class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                        >UCX_MEM_MMAP_HOOK_MODE=none</code
                      >
                      <strong>fixed our issue. </strong>We discussed that
                      <a
                        href="https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/24264"
                        target="_blank"
                        rel="noopener noreferrer"
                        class="underline font-semibold"
                        >investigation in the vLLM repository</a
                      >
                      and merged
                      <a
                        href="https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/32181"
                        target="_blank"
                        rel="noopener noreferrer"
                        class="underline font-semibold"
                        >a patch for the community</a
                      >.
                    </p>
                  </li>
                </ol>
                <h2
                  class="text-h3 font-mistral text-current mb-5 pb-5 border-b border-border-primary not-first:mt-16"
                >
                  <strong>What we learned.</strong>
                </h2>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  Modern software stacks are built on top of layers of dependencies, each adding
                  complexity and potential points of failure. While these abstractions greatly
                  enhance programmers’ productivity, it doesn’t fully insulate them from underlying
                  issues in the stack. That’s why it’s essential to be prepared to dig deep when
                  debugging. However, doing so in these environments is rarely straightforward,
                  especially when performance optimizations introduce subtle edge cases. UCX is a
                  great example of this. Its design prioritizes performance, but the way it
                  intercepts
                  <code
                    class="bg-surface-brand-secondary border border-border-primary rounded-sm px-2 py-px text-text-brand-8 dark:text-text-brand-7 text-base"
                    >mmap</code
                  >
                  calls can create hard-to-trace risks. This experience demonstrated once more how
                  challenging it can be to diagnose issues in deeply interconnected systems.
                </p>
                <p class="text-body-large text-text-primary mb-10">
                  This investigation also shows the importance of transparency and collaboration
                  when navigating performance-critical dependencies. We’re grateful for the
                  collaboration with the
                  <a
                    href="https://vllm.ai/"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >vLLM</a
                  >,
                  <a
                    href="https://github.com/ai-dynamo/nixl"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >NIXL</a
                  >
                  and
                  <a
                    href="https://openucx.org/"
                    target="_blank"
                    rel="noopener noreferrer"
                    class="underline font-semibold"
                    >UCX</a
                  >
                  teams in confirming and addressing this behavior. Their expertise was critical in
                  reaching a resolution, and we look forward to continuing our work together.
                </p>
